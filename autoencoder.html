Architecture

The whole autoencoder

The encoder squeezes the 128 samples through a dense layer into the code — k numbers — and the decoder rebuilds the window from them alone. A dense layer is drawn as one box, a bar per unit; the code as one small box per number. The output box shows the reconstruction (blue) over the input (grey) and the squared error underneath. Click anything — a unit, a code number, a sample of the output — to see how it is computed.

The encoder halves the length three times — convolution, ReLU, max pool: 128 → 64 → 32 → 16 — and a dense layer turns the last maps into the code; the decoder goes back the same way, repeating every value (upsampling) before each convolution. Every box is one channel's map. The output box shows the reconstruction (blue) over the input (grey) and the squared error underneath. Click a box for its arithmetic.

The encoder reads the 128 samples one at a time; only its last state goes into the code. The decoder gets the same k numbers at every step and has to unroll the window from them; a linear readout turns its state into the sample. Each box holds a whole layer: a row per unit, a column per step, orange positive, blue negative. Click a row at a step for the cell's gates at that moment.

No bottleneck here: the window is cut into 16 patches of 8 samples, some patches are replaced by a learned [MASK] vector (hatched), and the Transformer fills them in from the rest. Each box is a row per dimension, a column per token; the squares above are the attention maps. To score a window it runs four times, hiding every fourth patch each time, so every patch is predicted once without being seen. Click a token for its arithmetic, or a sample of the output to see the pass that hides its patch.

Experiment · how many numbers does a window need?

Trains a fresh autoencoder of the current kind for every code size k = 1, 2, 3, 4, 8, 16 and measures how well it rebuilds clean mains and how well its error separates the disturbances. A clean 50 Hz window is a sine of fixed frequency: amplitude and phase — two numbers — describe it.

Experiment · autoencoder or classifier: who notices the unknown?

Hold some classes out. A CNN classifier (three layers of 8 filters) learns the remaining classes with their labels; a fresh autoencoder of the current kind learns the very same windows without them. Then both see the test set, held-out classes included. A softmax always picks one of the classes it knows, so the classifier needs a separate novelty score — maximum probability, energy or the distance to its training windows — while the autoencoder just reports how badly it rebuilds the window.

Experiment · learning from a handful of labels

The autoencoder learned without labels. Is its code a good starting point when only a few windows are labelled? A logistic regression on the code of the current network against the same on the raw 128 samples and on their magnitude spectrum, and a CNN trained from scratch — all with the same 1–20 labelled windows per class. Train the autoencoder on every checked class first: one that has only seen clean mains has no reason to describe the disturbances.

Things to try

  • Train on clean mains only and watch the AUC table: every disturbance the decoder cannot rebuild scores high. With all 8 classes, k = 2 and 40 epochs the MLP autoencoder reached a mean AUC of 0.98–0.99 over two runs, the convolutional one 0.95–0.99 — without a single label.
  • The hardest classes are the frequency drifts (over, under): a slightly faster or slower sine is still a sine, and the decoder rebuilds it almost as well (AUC 0.87–0.99 depending on the run). Spikes, sags, bursts, ripple and harmonics sit at 1.00.
  • Pick a disturbed example in the Inspector and look at the red error band under the output: it peaks where the disturbance is. For the MLP the largest error fell inside the disturbance in 97–100% of the test windows, for the convolutional autoencoder in about 90%.
  • Set k = 2 and watch the latent space: clean windows lie on a ring — the two numbers have learned amplitude and phase, the angle around the ring being the phase. With k = 1 there is no room for both: the error on clean mains stays about 40 times larger and the mean AUC drops to about 0.6. Run the sweep below to see the knee.
  • Switch Train on to every checked class. The code now has to describe the disturbances as well — but at k = 2 there is little room, so the mean AUC only fell to about 0.97; the drifts lose most.
  • Tick denoising with k = 8: the network gets a noisier copy and must return the clean signal. At an added noise of 0.1 the MLP raised the SNR from 16.5 to 26.6 dB — better than a 5-point moving average (23.0 dB). The convolutional autoencoder reached 22.2 dB, no better than the simple filter.
  • LSTM AE — the same job, one sample at a time: the encoder has to carry amplitude and phase through 128 steps, and the decoder has to unroll a 50 Hz cycle from a constant input. It is slower (about 0.6 s per epoch against 0.02 s for the MLP) and less steady: after 40 epochs four runs gave a mean AUC of 0.81–0.95, a GRU 0.93. Watch the clean error — runs that stayed above about 0.009 stayed below 0.9 AUC. One run of 100 epochs passed 0.99 on the way (0.96 at the end).
  • Transformer MAE has no bottleneck at all: it fills in hidden patches from the visible ones. Because each patch is predicted from its neighbours, its error sits right on the disturbance — 94–100% of the windows, the best of the four. After 40 epochs (50% masked) three runs gave 0.82–0.97, after 100 epochs 0.94–0.97. Hiding 75% of the patches in training is too much (0.71); 25% worked as well as 50%.
  • Tick VAE with k = 8 and press Decode 4 random codes. A plain autoencoder never learned what a random code means: only 38% (MLP) or 41% (conv) of the energy of what it decodes is a 50 Hz sine. With the VAE (β = 0.01) it is 98% and 93% — every random code gives a mains-like window. The price is detection: the mean AUC fell from 0.99 to 0.91 (MLP) and from 0.98 to 0.89 (conv). At k = 2 even the plain MLP decodes sines (99%): with two numbers there is nowhere else for the decoder to go.
  • Who notices the unknown? With 5 classes and the sag held out (20 epochs), the autoencoder's error gave an AUC of 0.78–0.84 at k = 2 and 0.93 at k = 8; the classifier's max-softmax 0.83–0.88, its energy 0.78–0.97, k-NN 0.43–0.61. With all 8 classes and sag + EMI burst held out, energy did best (0.76–0.92) and the autoencoder reached only 0.61–0.65: having learned to rebuild spikes and ripple, it rebuilt the bursts just as well (AUC 0.43–0.46). An autoencoder that has to rebuild the known disturbances rebuilds much of what is new with them — it is at its best when it has seen only normal data. The classifier has its own trouble here: clean, over- and underfrequency look almost alike in 40 ms (52–66% accuracy with all eight).
  • Compression. The MLP autoencoder with k = 2 (clean mains, 40 epochs) at 5–6 bits per number — 1.25–1.5 bytes a window — rebuilt clean windows at 26.1–26.6 dB, as close to the noise-free signal as sending every sample (128 bytes, 25.0 dB: the background noise goes along) or every 4th (32 bytes). But sending the amplitude and phase of a 50 Hz sine in 2 bytes gives 42.5 dB: when you know the signal is a sine, the model written by hand wins. Over all classes the code reaches only 18 dB (every 2nd sample 23.5): what the code cannot carry is the disturbance — the anomaly itself.
  • Few labels — a negative result. With 8 classes, logistic regression on the code stayed at 12–33% (MLP, conv and MAE, 1–20 labels per class) — no better than on the raw samples (13–27%) or a CNN from scratch (14–30%), chance being 12.5%. On the magnitude spectrum it reached 49–55% with one label per class and 79% with 20. To rebuild the window the code has to hold amplitude and phase — exactly what does not tell the classes apart; the spectrum drops the phase by construction. The MAE's averaged tokens came closest (28–33% at 20 per class).
